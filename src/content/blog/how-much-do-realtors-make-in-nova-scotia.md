"How much do realtors make?" is the first question almost everyone asks me when they're thinking about this career. It's the right question to ask, and the honest answer is: it depends almost entirely on you. Here's how the money actually works in Nova Scotia.

## How do realtors get paid?

Realtors earn commission, a share of the sale price that's paid when a deal closes. There's no hourly wage and usually no salary.

The commission on a sale is negotiated between the seller and the listing brokerage, and it's often shared with the brokerage that brings the buyer. Your share then goes through your own brokerage, which keeps a portion under your agreement with it (the split) or charges you fees instead.

## A worked example

These are **example numbers only**. Commission is negotiated on every listing, and every brokerage's split is different.

| Step | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Sale price | $400,000 |
| Commission to your side of the deal (example 2%) | $8,000 |
| Your brokerage's share (example 30%) | −$2,400 |
| Your gross commission | $5,600 |

From that $5,600 you still pay your own income tax and CPP, and your business costs: marketing, your car, your phone, and your yearly NSAR, CREA and insurance fees. That's why one sale is not one paycheque.

## What do realtors make in Nova Scotia on average?

Job Bank, the federal government's employment site, reports these annual figures for real estate agents and salespersons in Nova Scotia, based on the 2021 Census:

| | Low | Median | High |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nova Scotia | $36,193 | $51,600 | $152,000 |
| Canada | $32,867 | $58,400 | $178,000 |

By region, the median ranges from about $36,000 on the North Shore to $55,200 in the Annapolis Valley and Cape Breton, with Halifax at $51,200 and the Southern region at $50,800.

Two things to keep in mind. These figures include experienced agents, so they're higher than a typical first year. And the gap between the low and the high is huge. That spread is the nature of commission work: a small number of agents earn a lot, and many earn modest incomes.

## Why is the first year usually the leanest?

Because you're paid at closing, not when you start working. A new agent spends the first months building a list of people who know they're in real estate, holding open houses, and learning the paperwork. A buyer you meet in March may not buy until summer, and closing comes weeks after that.

That's why I tell everyone considering this career to have six to twelve months of living costs saved before they start. Our [licensing cost calculator](/tools/licensing-cost-calculator/) adds up the course, fees and that savings cushion for you.

## What makes the difference in income?

After 25 years of watching agents succeed and struggle, the pattern is consistent. The agents who earn well:

- **Talk to people every day.** Real estate is a referral business, and your income follows the size of your network.
- **Stay in touch with past clients.** Most repeat and referral business comes from people you've already helped.
- **Treat it like a business.** They track their numbers, budget for slow months, and set aside tax money from every cheque.
- **Pick a brokerage that supports them.** Training, a broker who answers the phone on a live deal, and tools that cut paperwork all matter. Here are [10 questions to ask a brokerage before you sign](/blog/questions-to-ask-a-brokerage-before-you-sign/).

## Is it worth it?

For the right person, yes. Few careers let you set your own schedule and grow your income with your effort. But it's a business, not a job, and the first year tests your patience and your savings.
